# Chapter 2 research and correction notes

The primary textual source is `H:/Ancient Japan History/ch2/ch2 - raw OCR.txt` (24,092 whitespace-delimited words), checked visually and page by page against `ch2.pdf` (55 PDF pages, printed pp. 108–162).
The manuscript follows the source's substantive sequence but does not preserve its bibliographic apparatus, obsolete national framing, or claims that later research has weakened.

## Editorial corrections applied

- The narrative uses the current English forms Baekje, Goguryeo, and Gaya while noting the older source forms Paekche, Koguryo, Kaya, and Mimana where needed.
- Gaya is treated as a changing confederated field of autonomous polities, not a securely demonstrated Yamato colony.
- Silla absorbed Gaya polities progressively; Daegaya fell in 562, so the source's suggestion that a single “Mimana kingdom” disappeared immediately after 531 is not retained.
- The Gwanggaeto Stele and the Seven-Branched Sword are used as contemporary but politically interested evidence; neither is made to prove a permanent hierarchy between modern nations.
- Imperial-designated tomb names are given as traditional attributions, not as archaeologically demonstrated identities.
- The 478 memorial is paraphrased rather than quoted at length and is read as royal diplomatic rhetoric.
- Yōmei succeeded Bidatsu in 585 and died in 587; the source's sentence placing his enthronement in 587 is corrected.
- The formal transmission of Buddhism is described with the traditional 538/552 dating dispute rather than assigned to one certain embassy.
- Migrants and specialists are treated as historical actors whose circumstances ranged from invited mobility to dependency and coercion.

## Modern institutional checks

- [UNESCO: Mozu-Furuichi Kofun Group](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1593) and the nomination documentation confirm the fifth-century concentration, architectural variety, hierarchy, and extraordinary labor of the tomb clusters.
- [UNESCO: Sacred Island of Okinoshima](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1535) confirms ritual deposits beginning in the fourth century and continuing to the ninth in the context of exchanges among the archipelago, Korean Peninsula, and continent.
- [UNESCO: Gaya Tumuli](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1666/) describes multiple autonomous Gaya polities sharing cultural practices while cooperating, competing, and exchanging with neighboring states.
- [National Museum of Japanese History: Gaya—The History of Co-Existence in Ancient East Asia](https://archive.rekihaku.ac.jp/english/exhibitions/project/old/221004/index.html) emphasizes Gaya maritime trade, iron production, multiple polities, and reciprocal interaction with Wa.
- [National Museum of Japanese History: Japan–Korea relations in the Kofun period](https://archive.rekihaku.ac.jp/english/education_research/research/list/joint/2014/122.html) describes long, interactive exchange and the role of migrant groups in restructuring settlements on both sides of the sea.

These sources serve as targeted audit controls.
They do not replace the assigned OCR as the chapter's primary source.
