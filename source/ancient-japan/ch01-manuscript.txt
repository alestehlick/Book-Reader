SECTION 01.01 - Before Japan
The story begins before there was a country called Japan.
The long island chain held many communities, languages, and ways of life, but no court commanded them all and no border enclosed a Japanese people.
It is therefore safer to speak of the Japanese archipelago, and later of the communities that outsiders called Wa.
Only near the end of this chapter do we approach a political world from which the Yamato polity would emerge.
Even then, unity was an ambition rather than an accomplished fact.

The oldest Japanese books tell a different kind of beginning.
The Kojiki, compiled in 712, and the Nihon Shoki, completed in 720, lead the imperial line from the sun deity Amaterasu to Jimmu, the first human sovereign in their genealogy.
They place Jimmu's accession in 660 BC and describe his eastward advance to the Yamato basin.
These works preserve court memory, ritual claims, poetry, and political theology from a much later age.
They are indispensable evidence for the eighth-century court, but they are not contemporary reports of the distant prehistoric past.

Archaeology supplies a different archive.
Stone flakes, hearths, storage pits, shell mounds, plant remains, human bones, fields, weapons, and imported objects survive without explaining themselves.
Their dates and contexts can be tested, yet every interpretation remains an argument made from incomplete traces.
The written record begins from outside the archipelago, in Chinese dynastic histories concerned with embassies, tribute, geography, and political marvels.
These observers saw coastal envoys and reported polities, not an entire population.

This chapter keeps those archives distinct.
It uses material remains to reconstruct Paleolithic, Jomon, and Yayoi lives; Chinese testimony to glimpse the later communities of Wa; and Japanese chronicles to study retrospective ancestry.
Modern genetic evidence adds another history, one of population movement and mixture.
No single archive cancels the others, and none should be turned into a simple origin story for a modern nation.
Together they reveal an archipelago repeatedly made by movement across water.

SECTION 01.02 - The First Crossings
The earliest secure archaeological traces of modern humans in the main islands date to roughly thirty-eight thousand years ago.
Claims for a much earlier occupation once attracted great attention, but the Paleolithic hoax exposed in 2000 forced a severe reexamination of sites and methods.
Evidence earlier than about forty thousand years remains unconfirmed under the strongest standards of workmanship, stratigraphy, and repeated association.
That caution is not an absence of history.
It is a reminder that a trustworthy beginning must be earned layer by layer.

Lower sea levels changed the outline of East Asia during the last ice age, yet they did not turn every channel into dry land.
Hokkaido was connected northward through Sakhalin at times, while Kyushu lay closer to the Korean Peninsula.
The main islands formed a larger landmass, but deep or persistent straits still interrupted the routes from the south and toward offshore islands.
People entered by more than one path and adapted to sharply different environments.
The archipelago was a meeting ground, not the terminus of one marching column.

Obsidian makes some of these journeys visible.
Stone from Kozushima, an island south of central Honshu, appears at mainland sites from the opening centuries of the Late Paleolithic.
Obtaining it required purposeful sea travel and a return crossing over water beyond the horizon.
Farther south, human remains and sites in the Ryukyu chain show that voyagers reached islands that had never been joined to the continent by a simple land bridge.
Boats have vanished, but the distribution of stone and people preserves the fact of navigation.

The first settlers did not enter an empty stage with a fixed script.
They learned volcanic landscapes, river terraces, coasts, forests, and cold grasslands whose plants and animals shifted with climate.
Their descendants were not isolated from the continent forever, nor can every later inhabitant be traced to a single founding band.
Movement continued in both directions across the northern, western, and southern seas.
The earliest history of the islands is thus already an East Asian and maritime history.

SECTION 01.03 - Life in the Late Ice Age
Paleolithic communities moved through territories larger than any surviving camp.
Small groups returned to favorable terraces, river crossings, stone sources, and hunting grounds, leaving dense scatters in some places and only a few flakes in others.
Their tools included backed blades, trapezoids, points, scrapers, and later microblades set into composite implements.
Tool traditions varied by region and changed through time.
They were learned techniques, not an evolutionary ladder from crude to refined.

The great eruption of the Aira caldera in southern Kyushu, around thirty thousand years ago, spread ash across much of the archipelago.
It transformed habitats and created a chronological marker that archaeologists can follow over enormous distances.
People endured this unstable world by moving, regrouping, and altering what they made.
Cold phases favored large game and open-country resources in some regions, while coasts and forests offered different possibilities elsewhere.
No single Paleolithic diet can represent the whole chain of islands.

Hunting mattered, but the surviving stone points can distort our view.
Wooden tools, baskets, cordage, hides, gathered plants, and most prepared food rarely survive in acidic soil.
Women, men, children, and elders maintained camps and knowledge through labor that left little stone behind.
Seasonal judgment was as important as a sharp edge.
A community had to remember when nuts ripened, where animals passed, which shore was safe, and how distant groups could be reached.

By the final millennia of the ice age, microblade technologies linked the islands to broad northern and eastern Asian traditions.
Other tool forms and routes point toward the Korean Peninsula and the Pacific rim.
These resemblances do not prove that whole peoples moved whenever a tool changed.
Objects can travel through exchange, imitation, marriage, or the relocation of a few skilled makers.
The archaeological record shows contact and choice more securely than it shows named populations.

SECTION 01.04 - Pottery at the Edge of the Pleistocene
At Odai Yamamoto I in northern Honshu, charred residue on pottery has been dated to roughly sixteen thousand five hundred years ago.
Other early vessels across East Asia show that pottery was not simply invented after farming or permanent village life.
It appeared among mobile or partly settled foragers near the end of the last ice age.
The old equation of pottery with an agricultural revolution does not fit this evidence.
A container could change cooking long before a field changed the landscape.

The first pots were scarce, and their use must not be imagined from later abundance.
They could simmer fish, meat, nuts, and plants; render oils; soften foods; and hold meals that joined a group around a hearth.
Residues from early ceramics often point to aquatic resources, though uses differed by place and vessel.
Pottery was heavy and breakable, so even occasional production suggests repeated returns or shorter seasonal rounds.
It marks a change in practice, not an instant conversion to settled life.

Archaeologists call this opening ceramic phase Incipient Jomon.
The term Jomon means cord marking, but the earliest vessels display several techniques, and not every later Jomon pot carries the same decoration.
Period names are modern tools for ordering evidence.
They should not be mistaken for the names by which ancient people knew themselves.
Nor did one cultural boundary fall across Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikoku, Kyushu, and the Ryukyus at the same moment.

As the climate warmed after the last glacial maximum, forests expanded and coastlines moved inland.
New plants, fish runs, shellfish beds, and woodland animals supported more concentrated use of favored places.
The bow became prominent, pit dwellings more visible, and ceramic traditions more varied.
These changes unfolded over millennia and at different tempos.
The Jomon world emerged through accumulated experiments in mobility, food, storage, social ties, and ritual.

SECTION 01.05 - Many Jomon Worlds
The Jomon period endured for more than ten thousand years in much of the main archipelago.
Its conventional stages, from Incipient and Initial through Early, Middle, Late, and Final, organize changes in pottery and settlement.
Absolute dates vary by region and by the calibration of radiocarbon samples.
Northern Kyushu entered an agricultural Yayoi sequence centuries before some eastern and northern communities did.
A single national timetable therefore conceals the very diversity it is meant to explain.

Environmental zones encouraged different economies.
The rich deciduous forests of northeastern Honshu offered chestnuts, walnuts, acorns, game, and salmon.
The Pacific and Inland Sea coasts supplied shellfish and many kinds of fish, while southwestern communities drew on warmer forests and maritime routes.
Hokkaido and the Ryukyus followed histories connected to, but never identical with, those of central Japan.
Jomon was a family of regional lifeways joined by overlapping practices rather than one uniform tribe.

Pottery makes regional identity unusually visible.
Communities shaped clay with local tempers, built forms suited to cooking and serving, and marked surfaces with cords, shells, incisions, and modeled ornament.
Styles spread, met, and changed at their borders.
A vessel could be useful equipment, a display of skill, and a carrier of social memory at once.
Its decoration mapped networks of learning that cannot be reduced to political frontiers.

Population also rose and fell unevenly.
Site numbers suggest substantial growth in parts of central and northeastern Honshu during the Early and Middle Jomon, followed by contraction and reorganization.
Such counts do not translate neatly into a census, because one group could leave many sites and preservation differs by landscape.
Even so, they show that the density of life shifted dramatically over time.
The long Jomon sequence was dynamic, not a changeless age before history.

SECTION 01.06 - Houses, Settlements, and Memory
Pit dwellings were built by setting a floor below ground level and raising a roof on posts.
Their shapes, hearths, and rebuilding histories varied, but many formed clusters around open spaces.
At some sites, storage pits, graves, refuse zones, and paths organized a settlement beyond the household.
A village was not merely a row of shelters.
It was a remembered arrangement of work, ancestry, hospitality, and return.

Sannai Maruyama in northern Honshu reveals the scale that Jomon settlement could reach during portions of the Early and Middle periods.
Hundreds of building traces accumulated there over many generations, with long houses, storage pits, graves, and large post settings.
The site was not necessarily a dense town occupied at the same size throughout its history.
Its importance lies in repeated construction, far-reaching exchange, and durable attachment to place.
Monumentality could grow without a palace, army, or writing.

Settlement did not eliminate mobility.
People could maintain a central village while visiting fishing stations, hunting grounds, nut groves, clay beds, and stone sources.
Some houses were rebuilt in the same area; other sites were occupied briefly or seasonally.
The useful contrast is therefore not between wandering and settled people.
It is between different schedules of movement, each anchored by particular relationships and resources.

Large structures required cooperation, but they do not by themselves prove a ruling class.
House size, grave goods, diet, and access to valued materials sometimes reveal differences, yet most Jomon communities lack the sharp hierarchy of later chiefly societies.
Authority may have depended on age, ritual knowledge, skill, generosity, or the ability to connect households.
Leadership could be real without becoming an office inherited through a state.
The evidence invites a spectrum of arrangements rather than a choice between equality and kingship.

SECTION 01.07 - A Managed Abundance
Jomon subsistence is often called hunting and gathering, but the phrase can sound more passive than the evidence allows.
People burned vegetation, tended useful trees, scheduled harvests, processed toxins, stored food, and reshaped habitats around settlements.
Chestnut stands near major sites may reflect deliberate encouragement and transplantation.
This was not field agriculture on the Yayoi model, yet it was skilled landscape management.
The border between wild and cultivated was a zone of practice.

Nuts were abundant but laborious.
Acorns often required leaching; chestnuts and walnuts had to be collected, dried, cracked, and stored against damp and animals.
Grinding stones, pits, and charred remains reveal work distributed across seasons.
Plant food could support a community only through planning and repeated knowledge.
Its archaeological invisibility once made meat seem more dominant than it was.

Coastal shell mounds preserve another unusually rich archive.
They contain shell, fish bone, mammal bone, tools, ornaments, hearth waste, and sometimes burials.
Their contents record changing shorelines as well as meals.
In the Kanto region, the warm-water Jomon transgression flooded low valleys and drew the sea deep into areas now inland.
Later falling seas moved estuaries and forced communities to revise familiar routes.

Fishing technologies included hooks, harpoons, nets, weirs, dugout canoes, and knowledge of tidal and seasonal movement.
Some communities pursued offshore species; others concentrated on rivers, bays, or shell beds.
Dogs assisted hunting and sometimes received careful burial, suggesting relationships beyond simple utility.
Food linked people to animals, plants, waters, and places through customary obligations we can only partly recover.
Abundance was produced by attention, not granted automatically by nature.

SECTION 01.08 - Exchange Without a State
Jomon exchange carried materials far beyond their sources.
Obsidian moved from islands and mountain deposits, jade from the Itoigawa region traveled widely, and asphalt, amber, shell ornaments, and finished objects crossed regional boundaries.
No royal road or merchant bureaucracy directed these movements.
They passed through visits, partnerships, marriage, seasonal gatherings, gifts, and chains of exchange.
Distance reveals connection but not the exact social contract behind it.

Some materials mattered because they were technically excellent.
Obsidian fractures into a sharp edge, while asphalt can haft a point and repair a vessel.
Other objects drew value from color, rarity, origin, workmanship, or the histories carried with them.
A jade pendant was more than transported stone once it had moved through hands and ceremonies.
Exchange joined practical need to reputation and memory.

Craft specialization existed in degrees.
Fine lacquer, complex basketry impressions, shell bracelets, carved objects, and elaborate pottery demanded trained judgment and time.
A skilled maker might work for a household, a seasonal gathering, or a wider network without belonging to a permanent occupational class.
The same object could circulate between economic and ritual settings.
Modern categories divide what ancient practice may have kept together.

Networks also transmitted styles and information.
Pottery forms could move without clay vessels traveling, because visitors remembered techniques or potters relocated.
Marriage linked communities biologically and politically long before written treaties.
News about harvests, conflict, dangerous routes, and ceremonies followed the same paths as valued goods.
The archipelago was socially connected at many scales, even though no center controlled the whole.

SECTION 01.09 - Bodies, Ritual, and Belonging
Jomon graves usually occur within or near places of living.
The dead were flexed, extended, placed in pits or jars, sometimes sprinkled with pigment, and occasionally accompanied by ornaments or tools.
Treatment varied by age, region, and period.
Burial close to houses made ancestry part of the inhabited landscape.
It also gives archaeologists a biased sample, since acidic soils destroy bone across much of the archipelago.

Some people underwent intentional tooth removal or filing according to patterned rules.
These modifications were visible signs carried through life and may have marked age, marriage, affiliation, mourning, or ritual status.
The exact meanings remain debated.
Their disciplined repetition nevertheless shows that belonging could be inscribed on the body.
Identity was made through social acts, not merely inherited as biological descent.

Clay figurines called dogu appear in many forms, especially in eastern Japan.
Many emphasize faces, breasts, bellies, or limbs, and many were broken before deposition.
They have been interpreted as images of fertility, healing, ancestors, spirits, or ritual substitution.
No one explanation fits every figure across thousands of years.
What is secure is that modeling and breaking clay participated in consequential acts beyond ordinary cooking.

Stone rods, circles, arranged deposits, lacquered objects, and unusual buildings expand the ritual field.
Ceremony may have gathered dispersed communities and helped negotiate marriage, exchange, grief, and seasonal renewal.
Ritual was not a decorative layer placed over subsistence.
It organized relations among households and between humans and a more-than-human world.
Those relations helped communities endure without becoming identical to later shrines, clans, or imperial rites.

SECTION 01.10 - Contraction and Regional Change
After the Middle Jomon peak, many areas of central and northeastern Honshu show fewer or smaller settlements.
Climate became cooler and in some places less favorable to resources on which large communities had relied.
Coastlines changed, forests shifted, and local overuse may also have mattered.
No single environmental cause explains every region.
People responded through movement, new exchange ties, altered settlement, and different combinations of food.

Later Jomon communities often formed smaller networks while developing intricate ritual objects and cemeteries.
In some areas, collective burial and stone arrangements made affiliation visible across households.
The reduction of settlement size did not mean cultural impoverishment.
It could concentrate social investment in gatherings, mortuary practice, and portable objects.
Complexity changed its form.

Contacts with the continent never ceased.
In northern Kyushu, Korean Peninsula crops and artifacts appeared within Final Jomon settings before irrigated rice became dominant.
In Hokkaido, northern exchange connected communities toward Sakhalin and the Amur region.
The Ryukyus maintained maritime worlds oriented through their own island chains.
The approaching Yayoi transition was therefore one of several regional transformations, not a wave that struck an undifferentiated Jomon shore.

The word transition can also mislead by making one period seem destined to become the next.
Communities adopted new plants, tools, and customs selectively.
Some combined them with older practices for centuries, while others resisted or remained beyond the agricultural frontier.
What later archaeologists group as Jomon and Yayoi overlapped in time and space.
The boundary is a useful pattern in evidence, not a line ancient people crossed in a single step.

SECTION 01.11 - Rice Comes Through the Straits
Irrigated rice cultivation began in northern Kyushu by about the tenth century BC, earlier than the chronology once used in many surveys.
The first secure paddies, field channels, tools, and associated pottery belong to a system already developed through East Asian farming histories.
Rice and millet had moved from China into the Korean Peninsula long before they crossed to Kyushu.
Their arrival in the archipelago joined a chain of knowledge, crops, and people.

The crossing was short in distance but large in consequence.
Migrants from or through the southern Korean Peninsula brought farming techniques, new pottery practices, and forms of settlement and burial.
Local communities contributed environmental knowledge, labor, exchange partners, and established ways of living.
The resulting Yayoi societies were not copies of a continental original.
They were made in the archipelago through collaboration, competition, intermarriage, and adaptation.

A paddy was a social machine as much as an agricultural plot.
People had to clear and level ground, control water, maintain banks, coordinate planting, and protect a harvest that ripened at one vulnerable time.
Wooden spades, hoes, rakes, and harvesting knives multiplied the work that survives in wet soils.
Storage raised questions about contribution, distribution, and authority.
Rice created new possibilities for surplus, but no field produced a state by itself.

Agriculture spread eastward unevenly through western Honshu and Shikoku, then into central regions.
Cold climate, local ecology, and existing social choices slowed or redirected it in the northeast.
Foraging, fishing, and hunting continued within farming communities, while dry-field crops accompanied rice.
The Yayoi economy was never rice alone.
Its novelty lay in the growing weight of cultivated harvests and the institutions built around land and water.

SECTION 01.12 - The Yayoi Village
Many Yayoi settlements combined pit dwellings with raised-floor storehouses and carefully divided work areas.
Some stood beside paddies on low plains; others occupied defensible uplands.
Ditches enclosed a number of villages, especially during periods of tension.
The settlement became an arena where household labor met collective decisions about water, storage, defense, and ceremony.
Its plan reveals new coordination without proving that every village had a king.

At Yoshinogari in northern Kyushu, ditches, palisades, large buildings, storage, and burials accumulated in a major regional center.
The visible remains belong to several phases and should not be frozen into one simultaneous city.
Even so, the site shows how large communities could organize protected spaces and distinguish important compounds.
Similar processes occurred at different scales elsewhere.
Regional centers grew through networks of villages rather than appearing fully formed.

Violence becomes unusually clear in parts of the Yayoi record.
Human bones bear projectile wounds and other trauma, settlements burn, and ditches separate inhabitants from attackers.
Competition may have concerned water, land, stored grain, routes, and political allegiance.
Yet weapons in graves or ritual deposits do not always record combat.
A blade could display status or address supernatural power after it ceased to be practical equipment.

Burial expressed increasing difference.
Northern Kyushu communities used jar coffins, wooden coffins, stone settings, and graves supplied in sharply unequal ways.
Some individuals received bronze mirrors, weapons, ornaments, or other scarce goods, while neighboring burials remained plain.
Such contrasts suggest hereditary advantage and emerging rank in certain regions.
They do not imply one uniform class system across the Yayoi world.

SECTION 01.13 - Metal, Prestige, and Sacred Power
Bronze and iron reached the archipelago through continental networks, but their histories diverged.
Iron edges improved woodworking and agriculture and eventually entered many practical tasks, though early supply was limited and much material came through the Korean Peninsula.
Bronze was often directed toward ceremony and display.
Its value lay not only in strength but in controlled access to foreign materials, casting knowledge, and resonant form.

Bronze weapons enlarged beyond useful fighting proportions in parts of western Japan.
Daggers, spears, and halberds became thin ceremonial objects, deposited in graves or caches.
In the Kinki region and farther east, dotaku bells developed from small functional models into large, richly decorated bronzes.
Many were buried away from settlements without accompanying human remains.
Their sound, images, and concealment suggest rites tied to community and agricultural renewal.

The distributions of weapon-shaped bronzes and dotaku once encouraged maps of rival cultural blocs.
Those patterns are real but shifting, and objects crossed their apparent boundaries.
They may mark networks of ritual practice, production, exchange, or alliance rather than fixed ethnic territories.
Later standardization and wider circulation accompanied the growth of broader political relationships.
Material style can reveal a political field without naming its rulers.

Chinese mirrors carried special authority.
Some arrived as diplomatic gifts or trade goods; others were copied in the archipelago.
Their gleaming surfaces, foreign inscriptions, and association with elite graves made distant connections visible.
Control over imported iron, bronze, beads, and prestige objects helped leaders convert external relationships into local standing.
Continental diplomacy and village hierarchy were becoming parts of the same system.

SECTION 01.14 - Migration and Admixture
The Yayoi transformation involved the movement of people as well as ideas.
Skeletal differences in northern Kyushu, archaeological links across the straits, and ancient DNA all support migration from continental East Asia through the Korean Peninsula.
The scale and timing varied by region.
There was no single invasion that replaced every Jomon community, and no purely local adoption that left population history unchanged.

Genomes from ancient individuals reveal mixture rather than two sealed races.
Jomon-related ancestry was deeply divergent within East Asia, while later migrants contributed ancestry associated with Northeast and East Asian populations.
Recent models often distinguish three major ancestral components in present-day mainland Japanese populations.
The third is especially visible in people of the Kofun age and after, reminding us that migration continued beyond the Yayoi transition.

Ancient DNA also exposes regional coexistence.
Some Yayoi-period people in northwestern Kyushu retained predominantly Jomon-related ancestry even after farming and migrant communities were established nearby.
Culture and ancestry did not travel in perfect lockstep.
A person could use Yayoi pottery or live within a farming landscape without possessing one imagined Yayoi biological identity.
Communities mixed at different rates and under different social conditions.

Modern populations cannot be projected backward as unchanged descendants.
Ainu, Ryukyuan, and mainland Japanese people retain different proportions and histories of ancient ancestry, followed by many later movements.
Genes do not carry language, political loyalty, or culture as a package.
They can estimate biological relationships among sampled people, but samples remain scarce and uneven.
The strongest conclusion is plural formation: the archipelago's people were repeatedly remade through continuity and arrival.

SECTION 01.15 - The Communities Called Wa
Chinese texts bring a few communities of the archipelago into written history.
The name Wa appears in accounts written from the perspective of imperial courts and commanderies.
In AD 57, a ruler of Na in Wa sent an embassy to the Later Han court and received a seal, according to the dynastic history.
A gold seal found on Shikanoshima bears an inscription consistent with that grant, though its archaeological context was not scientifically recorded.
Text and object meet here with unusual force and residual uncertainty.

Another embassy arrived in AD 107 with gifts and people described as captives or slaves.
Such missions were acts of local as well as international politics.
A leader who received recognition and valuable objects from a Han emperor could display a relationship unavailable to rivals.
The Chinese court, for its part, translated distant communities into its own language of tribute and rank.
Neither side necessarily understood the transaction in exactly the same way.

The third-century account known as the Gishi Wajinden describes many countries of Wa reached by sea from the Korean commandery of Daifang.
Its itinerary, distances, offices, customs, and marvels have been debated for centuries.
It distinguishes more than thirty polities and gives special attention to a federation centered on Yamatai.
This was not yet a description of a unified Japan.
It was a report about a fractured but diplomatically connected political world.

The text should be read neither as transparent observation nor as fantasy.
Its compilers used reports from envoys, conventions of Chinese ethnography, and the strategic concerns of the Wei court.
Some details can be compared with archaeology, while others resist identification.
The famous route to Yamatai cannot be plotted by treating every figure as a modern survey measurement.
Written testimony opens a narrow window, shaped by the frame of the observer.

SECTION 01.16 - Himiko and the Threshold of Yamato
According to the Wei history, prolonged warfare among the countries of Wa ended when they agreed to set a woman named Himiko over them.
She was said to serve spiritual powers, to remain secluded, and to communicate through a male attendant.
Her younger brother assisted in government, while guards and women attendants surrounded her residence.
The account presents sacred authority and political mediation as inseparable.
It does not tell us how her position worked from village to village.

Himiko sent envoys to Wei in 238 and received titles, bronze mirrors, cloth, and other gifts.
The embassy placed her federation within the diplomacy created by warfare among Wei, Wu, Shu, and the Korean polities.
Recognition from Wei strengthened Himiko's claims, while her mission extended Wei's symbolic reach across the sea.
This was an East Asian political transaction, not an isolated episode in a national chronicle.
Imported prestige and local coalition building had converged.

After Himiko died, the text reports a large burial mound, renewed disorder under a male ruler, and restored peace when a young female relative named Iyo or Toyo was installed.
Archaeology cannot securely attach Himiko's name to a particular tomb.
The competing locations proposed for Yamatai, especially northern Kyushu and the Yamato basin, depend on disputed readings of route, chronology, and material power.
The uncertainty should remain visible.
It is part of the evidence, not a flaw to be concealed.

During the third century, very large keyhole-shaped mounds and standardized elite objects began to link powerful groups across central and western Honshu and northern Kyushu.
That Kofun transformation belongs to the next chapter, as does the firmer appearance of the Yamato polity.
Here the threshold is enough.
Across tens of thousands of years, communities had navigated, exchanged, settled, farmed, fought, mixed, and negotiated ever wider alliances.
Japan did not awaken fully formed; a new political order gathered slowly from an ancient maritime world.
